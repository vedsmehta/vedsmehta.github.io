Most AI safety startup ideas die on one of two rocks.

The first is that the natural customer is a frontier lab. Labs build safety
infrastructure in house, hire the founders instead of buying the product, and
have no procurement path for a seed stage vendor touching model weights. An idea
whose only buyer is a frontier lab is a hiring pipeline, not a company.

The second is that the natural customer is a regulator. Regulators specify, they
rarely pay. The money arrives one step later, when the regulated party has to
prove compliance.

Agent security is the first category in this space that clears both.

## the change

Loss of control over agents is turning from a research concern into an
operational one, with a named threat, a named clock, and a buyer who is already
bleeding.

## what it makes valuable

Runtime containment. Egress control. Spend limits. Kill switches that actually
work under adversarial conditions rather than in a demo. And on the other side
of the wire, the ability to tell an authorized agent from a hostile one, and to
know which human is accountable for it.

## why now

*We Must Pace the Frontier* names sandbox escape as a capability checkpoint,
meaning a threshold past which a model needs certification before it ships. It
puts six to twelve months on a misaligned swarm capable of running a persistent
internet wide botnet, with damages in the hundreds of billions, and it cites an
incident that has already happened rather than a hypothetical.

The important part for this thesis is that enterprises do not need the
regulation to arrive before they start paying. They are running agents now, they
cannot see what those agents touch, and that is a present tense budget line
rather than a future one.

## the kill condition

If the major cloud providers ship adequate agent perimeter controls as a default
platform feature by **mid-2027**, the category collapses into a checkbox and the
independents lose. Watch the platform releases, not the funding announcements.

## confidence

Higher than T1 on the demand. Lower than T1 on defensibility.

I believe the spending is real. I am much less sure that any of it accrues to a
startup rather than to Amazon, Microsoft and Google, and a thesis that is right
about the market and wrong about who captures it is not a useful thesis. That
tension is what the first memo is for.
